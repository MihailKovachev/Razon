# Topology

The goal of the field of **topology** is to define the notions of "closeness", "vicinity" and "surrounding" for the elements of a [set](../Set%20Theory/Sets.md) without an explicit notion of distance which can be quantified numerically. There are several ways to do this, each with its own advantages and disadvantages. Conceptually, all of them express the same thing but do so via different mathematical objects.

The standard in modern mathematics is to define a **topology** as a [collection](../Set%20Theory/Collections.md) with additional properties. Depending on precisely which properties are required, the elements of this [collection](../Set%20Theory/Collections.md) are called either [open sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Open%20Sets.md) or [closed sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Closed%20Sets.md) (specifically, [open sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Open%20Sets.md) are used nowadays). The advantages of this approach are that it requires fairly few axioms (three in each case) and that some theorems can be stated in terms of [open sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Open%20Sets.md) or [closed sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Closed%20Sets.md) with fewer words. However, this approach is abstract and completely hides how [topology](./Topology.md) is meant to be the study of closeness and surroundings. The fact that it is standard today owes to a group of French mathematicians known as the Bourbaki group.

By far the most intuitive way to define closeness is via [neighborhood systems](./Topological%20Spaces/Neighborhoods.md) (it is already in the name). The main disadvantages of this approach is that it requires four axioms instead of three and a few more words when stating certain theorems and definitions. What it lacks in conciseness, however, is completely overshadowed by the geometric and linguistic intuition it provides. 

The approaches of [neighborhood systems](./Topological%20Spaces/Neighborhoods.md), [open sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Open%20Sets.md) and [closed sets](./Topological%20Spaces/Closed%20Sets.md) are equivalent to one another in the sense that given the terminology used in one of them, it is possible to define the terms used in the other two in a consistent way which yields the same mathematical results. In fact, the field of [topology](./Topology.md) is riddled with terminology from all three approaches. The real question is which to use as a starting point.

There are also additional, still equivalent, ways to define closeness, but they are even more abstract, non-intuitive and non-standard.
