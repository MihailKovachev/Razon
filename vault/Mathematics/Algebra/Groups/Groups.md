# Groups

>[!DEFINITION] Definition: Semigroup
>
>A **semigroup** $(H, v)$ consists of a [set](../../Set%20Theory/Sets.md) equipped with an [operation](../../Analysis/Functions/Functions.md) $v: H \times H \to H$ which is **associative**:
>
>$$
>v(v(f, g), h) = v(f, v(g, h)) \qquad \forall f, g, h \in H
>$$
>

>[!DEFINITION] Definition: Abelian Semigroup
>
>A [semigroup](#Semigroups) $(H, v)$ is **abelian** or **commutative** if
>
>$$
>v(f, g) = v(g, f)
>$$
>
>for all $f, g \in H$.
>

>[!DEFINITION] Definition: Group
>
>A **group** $(G, v)$ is a [set](../../Set%20Theory/Sets.md) $G$ equipped with an operation $v: G \times G \to G$ which satisfies the following conditions:
>
>- Associativity: $v(v(f,g),h) = v(f, v(g,h))$ for all $f,g,h \in G$.
>- Existence of an identity element: There is some $e \in G$ such that $v(e,g) = v(g,e) = g$ for all $g \in G$.
>- Existence of inverse elements: For each $g\in G$, there exists some $f \in G$ such that $v(f, g) = v(g, f) = e$.
>
>>[!DEFINITION] Definition: Finite and Infinitr Groups
>>
>>A [group](./Groups.md) is **finite** if $G$ is [finite](../../Set%20Theory/Cardinality.md) and is **infinite** otherwise.
>>
>
>>[!NOTATION] Multiplicative Notation
>>
>>When this notation is used, we write $a \cdot b$ or $ab$ instead of $v(a, b)$ for $a, b \in G$. The identity element is denoted by $1$ and the inverse of an element $g \in G$ is denoted by $g^{-1}$.
>>
>
>>[!NOTATION] Additive Notation
>>
>>When this notation is used, we write $a + b$ instead of $v(a, b)$ for $a, b \in G$. The identity element is denoted by $0$ and the inverse of an element $g \in G$ is denoted by $-g$. We also write $a - b$ instead of $a + (-b)$ for $a, b \in G$.
>>
>

>[!THEOREM] Theorem: Uniqueness of Identity
>
>The [identity element](#Groupss) of a [group](.#Groups) is unique.
>
>>[!PROOF]-
>>
>>Suppose the [group](#Groupss) $(G, \cdot)$ has to identity elements $e_1 \ne e_2$.
>>
>>Since $e_1$ is an [identity element](#Groups), we have:
>>
>>$$
>>e_1 \cdot e_2 = e_2 \cdot e_1 = e_2
>>$$
>>
>>Similarly, since $e_2$ is an [identity element](#Groups), we have:
>>
>>$$
>>e_2 \cdot e_1 = e_1 \cdot e_2 = e_1
>>$$
>>
>>We thus have:
>>
>>$$
>>e_1 \cdot e_2 = e_2 \cdot e_1
>>$$
>>
>>The left-hand side is by definition $e_2$ and the right-hand side is by definition $e_1$, so $e_2 = e_1$.
>>
>

>[!THEOREM] Theorem: Uniqueness of Inverses
>
>Each element in a [group](#Groups) has one and only one [inverse](#Groups).
>
>>[!PROOF]-
>>
>>Suppose there was some element $g$ with two distinct [inverses](#Groups) $g_1^{-1}$ and $g_2^{-1}$.
>>
>>Since $g_1^{-1}$ is an [inverse](#Groups) of $g$, we have:
>>
>>$$
>>g \cdot g_1^{-1} = g_1^{-1} \cdot g = 1
>>$$
>>
>>Similarly, since $g_2^{-1}$ is an [inverse](#Groups) of $g$, we have:
>>
>>$$
>>g \cdot g_2^{-1} = g_2^{-1} \cdot g = 1
>>$$
>>
>>This means that
>>
>>$$
>>g \cdot g_1^{-1} = g_1^{-1} \cdot g = g \cdot g_2^{-1} = g_2^{-1} \cdot g
>>$$
>>
>>and, in particular, we have:
>>
>>$$
>>g \cdot g_1^{-1} = g \cdot g_2^{-1}
>>$$
>>
>>We multiply both sides by $g_1^{-1}$ and apply associativity:
>>
>>$$
>>\begin{aligned}
>>g_1^{-1} \cdot g \cdot g_1^{-1} &= g_1^{-1} \cdot g \cdot g_2^{-1} \\
>>(g_1^{-1} \cdot g) \cdot g_1^{-1} &= (g_1^{-1} \cdot g) \cdot g_2^{-1}
>>\end{aligned}
>>$$
>>
>>Since $g_1^{-1}$ is an [inverse](#Groups) of $g$, we have $g_1^{-1} \cdot g = 1$ and so:
>>
>>$$
>>\begin{aligned}
>>(g_1^{-1} \cdot g) \cdot g_1^{-1} &= (g_1^{-1} \cdot g) \cdot g_2^{-1} \\
>>1 \cdot g_1^{-1} &= 1 \cdot g_2^{-1} \\
>>g_1^{-1} &= g_2^{-1}
>>\end{aligned}
>>$$
>>
>

>[!THEOREM] Theorem: Equations in Groups
>
>Let $(G, \cdot)$ be a [group](#Groupss).
>
>For each $a,b \in G$, the solutions to the equations
>
>$$
>ax = b \qquad \text{and} \qquad ya = b
>$$
>
>are
>
>$$
>x = a^{-1} b \qquad \text{and} \qquad y = ba^{-1}
>$$
>
>>[!PROOF]-
>>
>>We need to prove four things:
>>- (1) $x = a^{-1} b$ is a solution to $ax = b$. 
>>- (2) If $x'$ is also a solution of $ax = b$, then $x' = x$.
>>- (3) $y = ba^{-1}$ is a solution of $ya = b$.
>>- (4) If $y'$ is also a solution of $ya = b$, then $y' = y$.
>>
>>**Proof of (1):**
>>
>>We substitute $x = a^{-1}b$ into the left-hand side of the equation $ax = b$:
>>
>>$$
>>a(a^{-1}b) = (aa^{-1})b = 1 \cdot b = b
>>$$
>>
>>Thus, $x = a^{-1}b$ is a solution.
>>
>>**Proof of (2):** *The following proof was generated by AI and has not been human-verified. As such, it may contain mistakes.*
>>
>>Assume $x'$ is any solution to $ax=b$. Then $ax' = b$. We must show that $x' = a^{-1}b$.
>>
>>$$
>>\begin{aligned}
>>ax' &= b \\
>>a^{-1}(ax') &= a^{-1}b && \text{Left-multiply by } a^{-1} \\
>>(a^{-1}a)x' &= a^{-1}b && \text{by Associativity} \\
>>ex' &= a^{-1}b && \text{by definition of Inverse Element} \\
>>x' &= a^{-1}b && \text{by definition of Identity Element}
>>\end{aligned}
>>$$
>>
>>**Proof of (3):**
>>
>>We substitute $y = b^{-1}a$ into the left-hand side of the equation $ya = b$:
>>
>>$$
>>(ba^{-1})a = b(a^{-1}a) = b \cdot 1 = b
>>$$
>>
>>**Proof of (4):** *The following proof was generated by AI and has not been human-verified. As such, it may contain mistakes.*
>>
>>Assume $y'$ is any solution to $ya=b$. Then $y'a = b$. We must show that $y' = ba^{-1}$.
>>
>>$$
>>\begin{aligned}
>>y'a &= b \\
>>(y'a)a^{-1} &= ba^{-1} && \text{Right-multiply by } a^{-1} \\
>>y'(aa^{-1}) &= ba^{-1} && \text{by Associativity} \\
>>y'e &= ba^{-1} && \text{by definition of Inverse Element} \\
>>y' &= ba^{-1} && \text{by definition of Identity Element}
>>\end{aligned}
>>$$
>>
>>Since any solution $y'$ must equal $ba^{-1}$, the solution is unique.
>>
