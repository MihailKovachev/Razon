When sigma is the final letter of a word, it is written as ς. The other form is used in all other situations. 

For the most part, each letter always represents the same sound with the following exceptions:
- The letters α, ι and υ can indicate either a short or a long [vowel](#Vowels). Although this was not done in Ancient Greece, today a diacritic may be placed on top of these letters to indicate whether the pronounced [vowel](#Vowels) is short or long: a breve (ᾰ, ῐ, ῠ) indicates a [short vowel](#Vowels); a macron (ᾱ, ῑ, ῡ) indicates a [long vowel](#Vowels).
- When γ is followed by γ, κ, χ, or ξ, it is pronounced as [ŋ].

Certain combinations of letters, known as **digraphs** are used to represent different sounds and [diphthongs](#Vowels).

When two letters which usually form digraph are supposed to be treated separately, a diaeresis (◌̈) is placed on the second letter: Ἠσαΐας.

## Iota Subscript

The [long diphthongs](#Vowels) /aːi/, /εːi/, /ɔːi/ were rarely written as αι, ηι and ωι, respectively. Instead, the iota was usually written as a subscript under the first letter: ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ.

## Accent Marks

Three accent marks are used to indicate the [accent](#Accent) in a word:
- The acute accent (◌́) can occur on any [vowel](#Vowels) or [diphthong](#Vowels). When used on a [diphthong](#Vowels), it is always placed on the second letter of the respective digraph. If placed on a [long vowel](#Vowels) or a [long diphthong](#Vowels), it indicates that the pitch begins low at the start of the [[TODO|syllable]] and rises until its end.
- The circumflex accent (◌̃ or ◌̑) can only occur on [diphthongs](#Vowels) and [long vowels](#Vowels). It indicates that the pitch begins high at the start of the [syllable](#Syllabification) and drops until its end.
- The grave accent (◌̀) is used to replace the acute (◌́) when an [oxytone accent](./Orthography.md) is reduced.

## Breathings

When the [Athenians](TODO) adopted the [Ionian alphabet](TODO) in 403 BCE, they repurposed the letter *eta* as a vowel, leaving no way to denote the [h] sound. The sound, however, remained in speech. Around 200 BCE, the head librarian of the Library of Alexandria, Aristophanes of Byzantium (Ἀριστοφάνης ὁ Βυζάντιος), introduced two diacritics called **breathing marks** for denoting [h].

A **rough breathing** (**δασεῖα**) on a word-initial [vowel](TODO) letter indicates that the vowel is preceded by a [voiceless glottal fricative](TODO) [h]. A word-initial *rho* is always accompanied by a [rough breathing](#Breathings) which likely indicated that the *rho* should be pronounced as a [voiceless alveolar trill](TODO) [r̥]. 

Originally, the [rough breathing](#Breathings) looked like the left part of the letter "H" (├). As time progressed, people began slowly curving the symbol in handwriting until it evolved into the ◌̔ symbol. 

>[!EXAMPLE]- Example: Rough Breathing on Word-Initial Letters
>
>ὑπέρ / Ύπερ [hy.pér]
>

A **smooth breathing** (**ψιλή**) on a word-initial vowel letter indicates the absence of a [voiceless glottal fricative](TODO) [h] preceding the vowel. 

Originally, the [smooth breathing](#Breathings) looked like the right half of letter H (┤). As time progressed, people began slowly curving the symbol in handwriting until it evolved into the ◌̓ symbol. 

>[!EXAMPLE]- 
>
>ἐγώ [e.ɡɔ́ː] - I
>
>ὀφθαλμός [opʰ.tʰal.mós] - eye
>
>ἴσος [í.sos] - equal
>

Originally, the [breathings](#Breathings) were placed on top of the letters they accompanied. When modifying a [diphthong](./Phonology.md), the appropriate symbol was placed on top of the second letter. This practice continued for both uppercase letters as well as for lowercase letters after their invention. However, placing symbols above uppercase letters later required increased line height with printing presses which ultimately led to [breathings](#Breathings) being placed before uppercase letters to save paper. This practice, as well as the rule for [diphthongs](./Phonology.md), still remains to this day.

The letters rho and upsilon always take the rough breathing. If the word begins with a capital letter, the breathing is placed before it: Ὠ, Ὡ. If the word starts with a [digraph](#Orthography), the breathing is placed on the second letter: αἰτέω. If the [stress](#Stress) also falls on the initial vowel, then the breathing and the [accent](#Accents) are combined - ὤ, ὢ, ὦ, ὥ, ὣ, ὧ, Ὤ, Ὢ, Ὦ, Ὥ, Ὣ, Ὧ.

## Syllabification

Syllabification in [Classical Greek](./Classical%20Greek.md) follows relatively consistent rules, primarily based on the principle that consonants tend to begin new syllables whenever possible:
- Every syllable must contain exactly one vowel or diphthong. [Digraphs](#Orthography) always remain within a single syllable, as they represent a single vowel sound or a diphthong (e.g., οἶ-κος, εὔ-χο-μαι). The [iota subscript](#Iota%20Subscript) (ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ) does not form a separate syllable and is treated as part of the main vowel for syllabification.
- A single consonant between two vowels generally forms the onset of the *following* syllable (λό-γος, ἔ-χω).
- When two consonants occur together, they are usually divided, with the first closing the preceding syllable and the second opening the following syllable (e.g., γράμ-μα, ἀρ-χή). If the first consonant is a stop (π, β, φ, τ, δ, θ, κ, γ, χ) and the second is a liquid (λ, ρ), they generally stay together and begin the second syllable (πα-τρός, ἄ-θλη-μα).
- When three or more consonants occur together, the first consonant typically closes the preceding syllable, and the remaining consonants (if they form a permissible cluster) begin the following syllable (ἔν-δει-ξις, ἄν-θρω-πος).
- In compound words or words with prefixes, syllabification usually respects morpheme boundaries (συν-ά-γω, ἀπ-έ-χω). This means a prefix often forms its own syllable or completes the preceding one, rather than having its final consonant move to the next syllable's onset.

>[!DEFINITION] Definition: Short and Long Syllables
>
>A [[TODO|syllable]] is:
>- **short** if it contains a [short vowel](#Vowels);
>- **long** if it contains a [long vowel](#Vowels) or any [diphthong](#Vowels).
>

## Accent

[Classical Greek](./Classical%20Greek.md) had a [[TODO|pitch accent]] system. The accented [syllable](#Syllabification) in a word was pronounced with a higher pitch than the rest of the [[TODO|syllables]] and in writing is indicated by one of three different [accent marks](#Accent%20Marks). There are two possible patterns of accentuation for [syllables](#Syllabification) which contain a [long vowel](#Vowels) or a [long diphthong](#Vowels) - the pitch can initially begin low and rise until the end of the [syllable](#Syllabification) (indicated by the [acute accent](#Accent%20Marks) ◌́) or it can begin high and then drop until the end of the [syllable](#Syllabification) (indicated by the [circumflex accent](#Accent%20Marks) ◌̃ or ◌̑). 

>[!IMPORTANT] Important: The Rule of Limitation
>
>The [accent](#Accent) can only fall on one of the last three [syllables](#Syllabification) and there are only five possible ways to place it in a given word:
>- **oxytone** [σ.σ.σ́]: an [acute accent](#Accent%20Marks) (◌́) on the last [syllable](#Syllabification) - "ὀξύς", "τιθείς", "ἀγαγών", "οὐδείς";
>- **paroxytone** [σ.σ́.σ]: an [acute accent](#Accent%20Marks) (◌́) on the second-to-last [syllable](#Syllabification) - "τύχη", "παρθένος", "λελειμμένος", "νθάδε";
>- **proparoxytone** [σ́.σ.σ]: an [acute accent](#Accent%20Marks) (◌́) on the third-to-last [syllable](#Syllabification) - "ὅσιος", "βασίλεια", "μακρότερος";
>- **perispomenon** [σ.σ.σ̃]: a [circumflex accent](#Accent%20Marks) (◌̃ or ◌̑) on the last [syllable](#Syllabification) - "τιμῶ", "ἀργυροῦς", "ποιεῖν", "ἐμαυτῷ";
>- **properispomenon** [σ.σ̃.σ]: a [circumflex accent](#Accent%20Marks) (◌̃ or ◌̑) on the second-to-last [syllable](#Syllabification) - "σῆμα", "πολῖται", "ἀπῆγε", "ἐμαυτῷ", "δηλοῦμεν".
>
>
>If the last [syllable](#Syllabification) is [long](#Syllabification) (except when it contains one of the [diphthongs](#Vowels) /ai/ or /oi/), then the [accent](#Accent) can only be [oxytone](#Accent), [paroxytone](#Accent) or [perispomenon](#Accent).
> 

>[!IMPORTANT] Important: The σωτῆρᾰ-Rule
>
>If the last [syllable](#Syllabification) is [short](#Syllabification) (or contains one of the [diphthongs](#Vowels) /ai/ or /oi/) and the second-to-last [syllable](#Syllabification) is [long](#Syllabification), then the [accent](#Accent) cannot be [paroxytone](#Accent).
>

In certain situations, an [oxytone accent](#Accent) may be reduced to only a small rise in pitch or no rise at all. This is indicated by changing the [acute accent](#Accent%20Marks) (◌́) to a [grave accent](./Orthography.md) (◌̀).

## Elision

A [short vowel](#Vowels) at the end of a word can often be dropped if the next words begins with a [vowel](#Vowels) or [diphthong](#Vowels). This is know as **elision** and is reflected in writing using an apostrophe in place of the vowel. If π, κ or τ precedes the dropped vowel and the next word starts with a [rough breathing](#Breathings), the π, κ or τ becomes aspirated (turns into φ, χ, θ, respectively).

>[!EXAMPLE]- Example: Elision
>
>ἀπ᾽αὐτοῦ = ἀπ(ὸ) αὐτοῦ ("from him")
>
>ἀφ᾽ οὗ = ἀπ(ὸ) οὗ
>

## Crasis

When the final [syllable](#Syllabification) of a mono- or disyllabic word ends in a [vowel](#Vowels) or [diphthong](#Vowels) and the following word also starts with a [vowel](#Vowels) or [diphthong](#Vowels), the two may blend together into a single [syllable](#Syllabification).
